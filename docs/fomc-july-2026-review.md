## 1. The statement, in full

Released 2:00 p.m. EDT, July 29, 2026. Approved 9–3. Reproduced here because its
brevity *is* the news:

> The Committee decided to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at
> 3-1/2 to 3-3/4 percent, in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate. The
> Committee is continuing its policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking
> system.
>
> Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace despite elevated uncertainty that
> owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East. Productivity growth and capital
> investment are strong. Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the
> unemployment rate has changed little.
>
> Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in part
> reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors,
> including energy. The Committee will deliver price stability.
>
> Voting against the monetary policy action were Beth M. Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and
> Lorie K. Logan, who preferred to raise the target range for the federal funds rate
> by 1/4 percentage point at this meeting.

Four structural things are absent that were standard in pre-Warsh statements: no
"in determining the extent and timing of additional adjustments"; no "the Committee
is attentive to risks to both sides of its dual mandate"; no risk-balance sentence;
no dot plot commentary. In their place, one declarative: *"The Committee will deliver
price stability."*

**Implementation note (effective July 30, 2026):** IORB 3.65%; primary credit 3.75%;
standing repo 3.75%; ON RRP offering rate 3.50% with a $160bn per-counterparty daily
cap. The Desk is directed to buy **Treasury bills and Treasuries ≤3 years** as needed
to keep reserves ample, roll over all Treasury principal, and reinvest all agency
principal into bills. All Board actions unanimous — the 9–3 split is on the rate only.

**Why the balance-sheet language reads as it does:** the FOMC judged reserves had
reached "ample" at the December 2025 meeting and switched from runoff to reserve
management purchases in January 2026. Reserve balances are ~$3.1tn, total balance
sheet ~$6.7tn (~21% of nominal GDP), with ~$250bn of bills bought since January and a
projected ~$540bn of SOMA bill demand for the full year. This is directly relevant to
the fourth question Warsh says the Committee debated (§3, Q4): *how much accommodation
is coming from the balance sheet?*

The two named shocks in the statement — "the conflict in the Middle East" and "supply
shocks… including energy" — are the same event. US–Iran hostilities began late
February 2026; the Strait of Hormuz and a naval blockade of Iranian ports have been

fell ~5% to $80.34 after Trump said he had called off a planned strike. The Fed has
written a live geopolitical variable into its statement, and that variable moved 12%
in five sessions.

## 3. Warsh's press conference, mapped to what is actually happening

Full transcript: `FOMCpresconf20260729.pdf`. Warsh's second press conference as Chair,
eight and a half weeks into the job.

### 3.1 "There is no soft inflation target"

> "For some households, businesses, and market professionals, five years of high
> inflation have left a mistaken impression that's hard to shake—that the Fed's
> implicit inflation target was somehow above 2 percent… There is no soft inflation
> target; there is no soft implicit target—not on this Committee's watch. There's only
> a target, and it's 2 percent."

This is the load-bearing sentence of the whole appearance, and it is aimed at a
specific, measurable problem: **U. Mich. long-run expectations stuck at 3.3%** and a
term premium that has been widening. Warsh's framing is that a chunk of the inflation
problem is a *credibility* problem — a "revealed preference" reading by the public that
the Fed tolerates 3%-ish — and that fixing the expectation is a substitute for, or at
least a complement to, fixing the rate.

He made the three-pronged version of this explicit to Colby Smith (NYT):

> "One way, absent the tools that you reference, to ensure that we get there is ensure
> that expectations are centered around the right number… making clear expectations is
> one part of it. Making sure we demonstrate we're responsible for it—we're not
> blaming is another—and our policy tools, like you referenced, is the third."

**Tension worth naming:** rhetoric and expectations are prongs one and two; the rate is
prong three and it did not move. Three reporters (McKee, Saphir, Irwin) pressed on
exactly that gap, and the bond market answered it in real time (§4).

### 3.2 "Play the ball, not the referee" — the forward-guidance withdrawal

> "Market attention centered on real data and real economic developments… the
> reduction in forward guidance may have been a factor. Market participants are
> learning to play the ball, not the referee… the central bank need not always and
> everywhere be the center of attention."

And on his reaction function, the closest thing to guidance he offered:

> "Any central banker, especially a central banker where the labor markets are more or
> less at equilibrium—any central banker, when he or she sees underlying inflation
> moving higher—he or she is more inclined to tighten policy. Again, when you've
> achieved the other side of your mandate and you see underlying inflation falling,
> he's more inclined to loosen policy. That's my reaction function."

Context: the June 17 statement, his first, ran ~130 words against 300+ under Powell.
Forward guidance has been stripped entirely. Wall Street has responded by building its
own substitutes — CNBC has been reporting on "WarshGPT," desk-side models trying to
reconstruct a reaction function from a Chair who refuses to publish one. There is a
documented investor backlash to the communication overhaul.

Warsh explicitly frames markets as an *input*, not a constraint:

> "We're not going to be constrained by market prices… But if we're trying to land the
> plane and deliver 2 percent inflation and we take a very useful source of
> information and we get it all fogged up by giving it our own forecast—by providing
> rolling commentary—I can assure you that we're going to have less information."

### 3.3 He is reading the bond selloff as a policy substitute

> "Nominal and real yields are materially higher across the Treasury curve. In fact,
> some of the increases in market interest rates between FOMC meetings are among the
> most significant in the last two decades, ranking around the top decile or so."

And to Ed Lawrence, rejecting the word "pause":

> "If you were to try to force a description that this was a pause, I would say
> financial market prices would take the other side of that. Financial market prices in
> this intermeeting period—they didn't pause."

This is the most consequential analytical claim of the day: **that the 42-day
tightening of financial conditions did the work a 25bp hike would have done.** It lets
him hold rates while claiming policy is tightening. Neil Irwin (Axios) attacked the
premise directly — funds rate ~75bp below the 2-year yield, ~100bp below most Taylor
rule estimates, employment mandate met, inflation elevated: *why should rates not be
higher today?* Warsh's answer was that markets moved so the Fed didn't have to.

**The vulnerability:** if the curve rallies back — which is exactly what began on
August 7 — the substitute disappears and the argument for holding goes with it.

### 3.4 The "family fight"

> "I asked for a good family fight, and I got one. That's the purpose; that's the
> design feature."

> "This is a period of watchful thinking, not watchful waiting. And I think the score
> on that vote was unanimous."

He declined to characterize the dissenters' arguments ("I'll let the dissenters speak
for themselves") and pushed back on the "divided Fed" framing in his closing answer.
Three dissents in one direction is nonetheless the most hawkish dissent bloc since
September 2016.

### 3.5 The four questions the Committee actually debated

Warsh enumerated them, and they are the best available map of where policy goes next:

1. **"Has the past really passed?"** — implications of five years of above-target
   inflation for the current conjuncture (i.e. is there an expectations scar).
2. **Do different shocks differ in their effects on output and employment?** — pandemic
   supply chains, military conflict, energy disruption, tariff increases, AI capex.
3. **Are shock-driven price increases broadening?** — "The business capex boom… is
   driving up prices of memory and logic chips and associated AI infrastructure. Do
   those changes indicate a broader inflationary dynamic, or do we just focus on them
   because they are under the bright streetlight?"
4. **"How much accommodation are we getting from the balance sheet?"** — with rates as
   the primary instrument, what is the ~$6.7tn sheet contributing?

Question 3 is the newest and least priced. An AI-capex-driven semiconductor/power
price impulse is a *demand* shock wearing supply-shock clothes, and it is the one
inflation source a rate hike would actually bite on.

### 3.6 Data dependence, rejected

> "The historic problem with data dependence is the data and the dependence. We are not
> relying on any one individual piece of data as cover or as an excuse or as
> validation."

Asked directly whether the cool June CPI print explained the hold: **"in two words, not
much."** On the 2% target's measure, he affirmed PCE per the January strategy document,
then immediately hedged:

> "Some version of the Lucas critique, some version of Goodhart's law… when we talk
> about measures of inflation… we might make them such that they're not very good
> measures or very good objectives."

> "I'm looking at a broader set of inflation data than PCE… my lens is broader than
> that, even though the remit is quite narrow."

He also flagged that the January 2027 strategy statement is in play: *"Who knows, come
after next January, what we might say about strategy. I suspect the task forces might
have something to add."* That is a live risk of a **framework change** — plausibly
including the target's definition or measure — inside six months.

### 3.7 The five task forces

Announced at the June meeting; leadership named July 9. Official titles, per the
Board's [task forces page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/task-forces.htm):
**Communications, Balance Sheet Policy, Data, Productivity and Jobs, and Inflation
Frameworks** — collectively the "Chairman's Task Forces for Advancing Monetary
Policy". Fifteen outside experts, reporting to the FOMC. Named participants include Marc Andreessen,
former BoE Governor Mervyn King, Greg Mankiw, and Doug McMillon.

Mike McKee (Bloomberg) raised the conflict question — Andreessen's ~$25m in political
spending backing candidates opposed to stricter AI regulation, while co-chairing a group
assessing AI's economic effects. Warsh's answer:

> "The judgments we're making will be informed by—but not at all determined by—these
> outside groups… full disclosure, I've known almost all of them for a very long time."

He is checking in with all five before Jackson Hole and said the data task force is
already at work — relevant because he cited it when dismissing reliance on the June CPI
print.

### 3.8 Jackson Hole

> "I look at it like a blank piece of paper right now… If I could, in the high mountain
> air in Jackson, Wyoming, I'd like to also frame the big questions… Haven't made a
> decision whether it's going to be a big-picture speech or whether it's going to be a
> more traditional set up for all the action we're going to have between September and
> December."

Symposium is **Aug 27–29, 2026**, theme "Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments
and Policy." Note the phrase *"all the action we're going to have between September and
December"* — an unusually loaded aside from a Chair who has abolished forward guidance.

### 3.9 No trade-off between the mandates

> "I do not believe that price stability and full employment is an either-or
> proposition… if you want to do the most harm to the labor markets, you would run a
> period of high inflation that's variable."

Nick Timiraos (WSJ) pressed on transmission: if not through the labor market, then how? Warsh's
answer — rates work through lending, credit, confidence and FX channels; the balance
sheet through signaling and portfolio balance — and:

> "If the suggestion is somehow we're going to be fine-tuning aggregate demand so it
> catches supply, that's not my mental model. I don't think we're great in the
> fine-tuning business."

**This is the doctrine most exposed by the July jobs report** (§6). Warsh's willingness
to tighten rests on "labor markets are more or less at equilibrium." If the labor
market is in fact cracking, the no-trade-off claim gets tested nine days after he made
it.

## 4. Market reaction, July 29

- **30-year Treasury 5.21%**, +12bp — highest since 2007, a 19-year high. It moved from
  ~5.10% to 5.21% *during Warsh's remarks*.
- **10-year 4.67%**, +7bp.
- **WTI $84.46**, +6.6%, on Trump's "hard" strike comment against Iran.
- Long-end steepening read across the street as a term-premium/credibility move, not a
  growth move — CNN's framing was the bond market telling Warsh *"show us you mean it."*
  Yahoo Finance reported desks calling it an **"inflation credibility shock."**

The uncomfortable read: the Chair spent an hour asserting no soft target, and the market
demanded more compensation for holding duration while he did it. Warsh's counter — that
higher real yields *are* the tightening — is coherent, but it concedes that the price
of not hiking is a permanently higher term premium.

Immediately after the meeting, September hike pricing was quoted between 53% (Polymarket)
and as high as ~82% (CME FedWatch on some prints), with a wide venue-to-venue spread.

## 5. The dissents

First time since **September 2016** that three policymakers dissented in the same
direction. All three explained themselves publicly on **Friday, July 31**.

**Beth Hammack (Cleveland)** — inflation is "too high"; price pressures are
"broadening rather than fading" among businesses and consumers are "expressing despair":

> "Now is the time for the FOMC to act to speed the return of PCE inflation to our
> 2 percent objective and deliver on our commitment to price stability for the American
> people."

**Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis)** — risk management against entrenchment, with an explicit
off-ramp:

> "To manage against the risk that high inflation could become entrenched, I would
> rather tighten policy incrementally."

…adding that if inflation "durably fades," the Fed could "slow or pause rate hikes."

**Lorie Logan (Dallas)** — the "no restraint, no disinflation" argument:

> "Without any policy restraint, inflation will likely continue to trend above target
> until there's an unanticipated shock."
>
> "Modest action in the near term would reduce the likelihood of needing to take
> sharper action later."

All three lean on the cumulative record: consumer prices up **20.8% over five years** as
of June. Note this directly contradicts Hammack's read against Warsh's Question 3 —
she says broadening, he says maybe just "under the bright streetlight."

Note also the *composition*: three Reserve Bank presidents, no Governors. The Board
voted unanimously on every implementation action. The split is regional-president
hawks vs. the Chair and the Board.

## 6. What changed after the meeting — July 30 to August 10

This is the part that matters most for positioning, because the meeting's central
premise weakened within nine days.

**Jul 30 — Core PCE 3.3% y/y (June)**, down from 3.4%; headline 3.7%, +0.1% m/m. Second
consecutive soft inflation print. Confirms the June CPI signal Warsh refused to lean on.

**Jul 31 — All three dissenters go public** with the statements in §5, keeping a
September hike alive in the narrative.

**Aug 3 — Trump calls off a planned Iran strike** at Tehran's request; WTI −5% to
$80.34, Brent −4.7% to $83.77. The energy leg of the statement's "supply shocks"
sentence partially unwinds. Same day, the Dow rose ~900 points and the S&P closed above
7,700 for the first time.

**Aug 7 — The July employment report breaks the frame.** Payrolls **−23,000** against
+83–95k expected, with **−146,000 in revisions** to prior months. Private payrolls
+30,000; government −53,000 (local government, education, retail led declines; health
care still adding). Unemployment fell to 4.1% — but participation fell to 61.4%, a
five-year low, so the rate improved because the labor force shrank. Average hourly
earnings +$0.02, y/y down to 3.2%, lowest since May 2021.

Consequences:
- **September hike odds collapsed to ~44%** (hold ~56%); October ~58%; money markets
  moved the modal hike to **December** or later.
- **Treasury yields dropped**; short-dated Treasuries outperformed. The long-end move
  Warsh cited as the tightening substitute began reversing.
- **S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64**, +3.6% on the week — strongest week since
  April; Nasdaq +5.2% on a chip rebound. Three headwinds eased simultaneously: AI-demand
  confidence, lower oil, and reduced Fed-tightening fear. The dollar fell.

**The direct collision with the statement:** "Job gains have kept pace with the
workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little" was written on July 29 and
contradicted by −23k plus −146k of revisions on August 7. Warsh's entire hawkish
posture is conditioned on "a central banker where the labor markets are more or less at
equilibrium." That conditional is now in question — and so is the "no cruel choice"
doctrine of §3.9. Wage growth at a five-year low also removes the wage-price channel
from the dissenters' case.

Both the Warsh camp and the dissenters can claim the data. Softer inflation *and* a
soft labor market argue for holding; but falling participation with rising AI capex is
also exactly the supply-side story Warsh has been telling, in which case the labor
weakness is less demand-destruction than he'd need for comfort.

## 7. The political overlay

Warsh was confirmed **54–45**, the closest Fed chair confirmation in the modern era, and
sworn in at the White House on May 22, 2026 — the first such ceremony there since
Greenspan in 1987.

Trump has publicly ceded rate-setting — *"I'm going to let him do what he wants to do…
he's going to do a good job"* — while continuing to press for cuts, and has said he
would not have chosen Warsh if he wanted hikes. As of late June he eased public pressure
with inflation above 4%. An August 6 CNBC analysis argued that Trump *treating Warsh as
an ally* is itself the economic risk: not one of the twelve votes on July 29 was for a
cut, and the longer Warsh withholds the cuts the White House wants, the greater the
political pressure and the risk of renewed attempts to remove Fed officials.

The independence question is therefore asymmetric to the usual framing: the near-term
threat is not that Warsh is captured, but that the *perception* of alignment lets the
market discount his hawkishness — which is one clean explanation for a 30-year yield at
a 19-year high on a day the Chair swore fealty to 2%.

## 8. What to watch

| Date | Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Aug 27–29 | Jackson Hole, "Financial Innovation" | Warsh has said it may be a "big questions" speech *or* a setup for "all the action between September and December." He'll have polled all five task forces by then. |
| mid-Aug / mid-Sep | July & August CPI, PCE | Third and fourth consecutive soft prints would validate the hold; a reversal ("expect some payback next month" per one PCE tracker) revives the dissent. |
| Sep 4 | August employment report | The single highest-leverage release. Confirms or refutes the July break. |
| **Sep 15–16** | **FOMC** | Live in both directions. ~44% hike odds post-jobs, three standing dissenters, no forward guidance to lean on. |
| Rolling | Iran / Strait of Hormuz | Written into the statement. Oil moved 12% in five sessions around the meeting. |
| Jan 2027 | Statement on Longer-Run Goals & Strategy | Warsh has pre-flagged task-force input. Framework/measure change is a real tail risk. |

**Three specific things that would falsify Warsh's July framing:**
1. Long-end yields fully retrace the intermeeting rise → the "markets did the tightening"
   argument evaporates and the hold looks like a pause after all.
2. Payroll weakness persists into August/September → "labor markets at equilibrium"
   fails, and the no-trade-off doctrine faces its first real test.
3. Long-run inflation expectations move *up* from 3.3% despite the rhetoric → the
   expectations prong of the three-pronged strategy is not working, leaving only the
   rate.
